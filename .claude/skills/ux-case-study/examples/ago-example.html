Designing beyond the gallery walls

I set out to fix the Art Gallery of Ontario's online account. Three studies later, I found the real gap wasn't on the website. It was inside the gallery.

Overview

I set out to study an account. The answer was the whole visit.

1The problem

The AGO had an online account that no one had a reason to use.

It lived only at checkout and offered nothing beyond a ticket.

→

2The insight

Visitors didn't need a better account. They needed a better visit.

Their biggest frustrations happened inside the gallery, not online.

→

3The solution

Turn the account into a guide for the visit, after fixing the basics.

Five prioritized recommendations, presented Dec 6, 2025.

Part 011.1

Context

Three museums, three different promises

All three museums aim to connect people with art, culture and education, but each positions itself differently. The British Museum and The Met build for the world, with deep online collections and resources for researchers. The AGO builds for its own community, and its site is organized around visit planning, tickets and memberships.

Art Gallery of Ontario

The Met

That positioning makes the account more important for the AGO, not less. A museum that depends on its local audience and on memberships needs a way to keep in touch with visitors between visits.

Key takeawayThe AGO's business depends on repeat local visitors, so its account should be doing more than any peer's.

Part 011.3

So what

A hypothesis worth testing

The account was the most telling gap. It existed only inside the cart, and signing in changed nothing about the site. That led me to a hypothesis: visitors would want a richer account, with saved artworks, personalized recommendations and loyalty rewards.

Key takeawayThe AGO had an account, but no reason for anyone to use it.

Part 022.1

Who I spoke with

Recruiting showed the problem before the interviews did

I spoke with two student account holders, two tourism professionals who visit museums worldwide, and a former ROM member. My screener asked for account holders who had bought tickets in the last two years. I couldn't find enough of them.

I widened the criteria to anyone who'd used an account to visit. Even those participants had barely touched their accounts. That turned into a finding.

I wrote a separate discussion guide for each audience. Sessions were semi-structured: the guide was a starting point, and I followed each participant's lead with follow-up probes.

Read the discussion guideSample questions · 60-minute session · 6 sections+
1. Warm-up ~5 min
  • Could you tell me a little bit about yourself?
  • What do you do for fun? What does your typical week look like?
2. Visiting habits
  • How often do you go to the AGO? What made you start visiting?
  • How do you decide when you want to visit?
  • Has anything changed about the way you plan your visit or engage with the AGO over time?
3. Planning and tickets
  • Did you use the website to plan your visit? On which device?
  • Can you tell me about your most recent ticket purchase for the AGO?
  • How do you find out about upcoming exhibits and events? How do you prefer to get updates?
4. The account

Account holders

  • Walk me through the last time you used your AGO account.
  • Probe: What were you trying to do? How often do you use it?

Non-account holders

  • What, if anything, would make you want to create an AGO account?
  • What has made you not want to create one? Do you have any concerns?
  • What is your understanding of the benefits of an AGO account?
  • If you made one, what would you want to use it for, and how often?
5. Feelings and comparisons
  • How do you feel when you visit the AGO?
  • Could you tell me about your ideal online experience with the AGO?
  • How do your visits to other museums around the world compare?
6. Wrap-up ~2 min
  • Is there anything you'd like to add about your experiences?

Part 022.2

Finding

Every participant called the pass something different

The AGO offers accounts, memberships, and a free annual pass for visitors aged 14–24. Participants couldn't tell them apart. Several didn't know which ones they had, and every participant had their own name for the pass.

“I think that [the Free Annual Pass] is referring to the 14–24 free pass for students… I am not sure if I only get it for a year.”

Oliver (persona), student account holder

Key takeawayIf visitors can't name what they have, they can't see why they'd want more of it.

Part 022.3

Finding

Nobody asked for saved artworks. They asked for exclusivity.

The features I'd hypothesized barely came up. What participants expected instead was early access: news about exhibits before the public, ongoing perks and discounts. And they noticed that signing in made no difference at all.

“I don't see much of a difference between having an account and not having an account… I have the same access to everything on the website.”

Participant 5, student account holder

Key takeawayThe fix wasn't more features. It was a reason to sign in.

Part 022.4

Finding

Privacy has a price, and the AGO wasn't paying it

Participants didn't want another password. They saw no reason for the sign-up form to ask for their home address and phone number. One tourism professional happily downloaded an AGO app for an interactive exhibit, because it didn't make her create an account.

Yet the same participants said they'd trade their details for something worth it. Even free Wi-Fi was enough.

Part 022.5

Synthesis

Two visitors, two very different reasons to care

Oliver is a student who uses the free pass and visits with friends. Emily visits for work with tour groups, and compares the AGO with museums in Europe and Asia. Both left their visits confused about what the account was for.

Part 022.6

The thread

Visits are social. The account is not.

Nobody described visiting alone. Visits were family outings, trips with friends, hosting guests or work tours, and most participants bought tickets for their whole group. The AGO's tools assume one person, one ticket.

Key takeawayDesign the account for the group, not the individual.

Part 033.1

A detour

Turned away on day one

My approved plan was to recruit visitors in the AGO lobby. On the first day, staff stopped the research. Within days I recruited two participants through my network, moved the sessions to the Royal Ontario Museum and Nielsen Park Creative Centre, and added a Reddit netnography to keep AGO visitors' voices in the study.

The setback became a comparison: I could now see what the ROM did differently.

Read the observation and discussion guideThink-aloud visit · 60 minutes · from admission to leaving+
1. Warm-up ~10 min
  • Why did you decide to visit today? Have you been before?
  • How often do you visit other cultural institutions? What motivates you?
  • Do you have an AGO account, or one with any other institution?
  • How did you plan your visit? What do you expect from it today?
2. Admission

Observe

  • How do they buy tickets, and how easily do they find prices?
  • What format is the ticket: paper, digital wallet, screenshot?
  • Any confusion between memberships and passes? Any friction?
  • Do staff encourage an account or membership? Are personal details shared, for example for Wi-Fi?

Ask

  • What do you think and feel about the admission process?
  • Account holders: walk me through the last time you used your AGO account.
  • Non-account holders: what has made you not want an AGO account?
3. Moving through the museum

Observe

  • Where do they go first, and how do they know?
  • Do they use a map, brochure, signage, staff or their phone?
  • Do they take photos, scan QR codes or look things up?

Ask

  • How did you decide where to go today?
  • How do you feel about finding your way around?
4. Ending the visit
  • Observe: how do they know they're done? Do they visit the gift shop or mention coming back?
  • How satisfied are you with your visit today?
  • Is there anything you would change about the visitor experience?

Part 033.2

Finding

The map was there. Nobody could find it.

Interview participants told me they couldn't find a physical map at the AGO, or any information about the heART Rate Monitor, an interactive experience included with admission. When I visited, I saw why. The maps were small and tucked away, and the exhibit was hidden behind a wall.

At the ROM, maps and tours were everywhere, in several languages, with audio tours a QR code away.

Fact check · your slide is titled "at the AGO", but the photos on it are from the ROM, so the caption says ROM.

Part 033.3

Finding

Reading the back of the exhibit

In the China gallery, I asked a participant to find the name of one object. She spent a long time working down a list of text beside the case, trying to match it to what she saw. Then she realized we were standing at the back of the glass case. Nothing had told her.

Nearby, a pamphlet meant to explain a case of snuff bottles was broken, in English only, and not accessible.

Key takeawayVisitors want to learn. The museum has to hand them the information.

Part 033.4

Turning point

When the screen matched the shelf

At the ROM, an interactive map laid out exactly like the display case changed everything. My participant tapped through the objects and learned more in minutes than she had all visit. The match between what she saw and what the screen showed did the work.

But not every case had one, and several devices were broken, low-contrast, English and French only, and inaccessible.

Key takeawayInformation works best when it matches what's directly in front of you.

Part 033.5

Finding

People come to be surprised, and to reflect

The moments participants remembered were interactive, surprising or reflective: exhibits that asked them a question, or invited them to think about repatriation, reconciliation and who tells a story.

Prompts, stations and installations that held participants' attention longest

“Exhibition design is a simultaneously networked dialectical process, one which develops in a dialogue between verbal-conceptual and visual-representational rhetorical techniques. The focal point of this process of reflection is the individual and his or her intellectual and physical perceptual capabilities.”

Bertron, Schwarz & Frey, Designing Exhibitions (2012)12

Key takeawayThe best moments were interactive. The account could bring more of them.

What I found

Five insights that change what the account is for

Each insight is backed by at least two studies.

01

The account is a receipt, not a relationship.

It only works inside checkout, forgets you're signed in, and shows you the same site as everyone else. Even account holders couldn't say what it was for.

Competitive analysisInterviews
02

Nobody knows what they've signed up for.

Account, membership and pass blur together. Every participant called the free pass something different.

InterviewsContextual inquiry
03

Privacy is a price the AGO isn't paying.

Visitors will share their details for real value, like early access, perks or even Wi-Fi. Right now they get nothing back.

InterviewsContextual inquiry
04

Visitors want the museum to meet them at the object.

The hardest moments were in the gallery: hidden maps, lists to decode, the back of a case. Information worked when it matched what was in front of them.

InterviewsContextual inquiry
05

Museums are visited in groups. The account assumes you came alone.

Visits were family outings, friends and guests, and most people bought tickets for everyone. No tool helps them plan together.

Takeaways

What I'd carry into the next study

01

Let each study rewrite the question

I set out to design account features. Following the evidence led me to the whole visit, and kept me from recommending features nobody wanted.

02

Treat site access as part of the method

Being turned away on day one taught me to secure permission before fieldwork, not assume it.

03

Five interviews show patterns, not proof

Next: recruit long-term account holders, visitors with accessibility needs and families, and usability-test the redesign.
