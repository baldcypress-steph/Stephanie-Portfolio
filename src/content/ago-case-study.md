## Designing Beyond the Gallery Walls

*A mixed methods research project exploring the Art Gallery of Ontario's (AGO) digital and physical visitor experience*

| Role | Timeline | Methods | Tools |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sole UX researcher (academic project, advised by Prof. Jessika Tremblay) | Sept – Dec 2025 (4 months) | Competitive analysis, 5 interviews, 2 contextual inquiries, netnography | Microsoft Teams (interviews), Slack and Teams channels (recruitment) |

### TL;DR

The Art Gallery of Ontario (AGO) is one of Canada's largest art museums, serving local visitors, tourists, educators, students, and researchers. Over the course of 4 months, I conducted a mixed methods research project, beginning with a competitive analysis of the websites of the AGO, The British Museum and The Metropolitan Museum of Art, followed by 5 interviews and 2 contextual inquiries.

My competitive analysis found that the AGO's website lagged behind the British Museum and The Met in accessibility, forms, checkout and accounts. I went in expecting to recommend a more feature-rich account, but my interviews pointed to a more basic problem: **most visitors didn't see a reason to have an account in the first place.** Participants were unsure how accounts, memberships and passes differed, were wary of sharing personal information, and said they would only sign up for a clear, exclusive benefit.

Based on this, I recommended that the AGO first give the account a clear value proposition, and then extend it into the in-person visit, where participants wanted more out of their experience.&#32;

### How my thinking changed

| Phase | What I believed | What I learned |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Competitive analysis | The AGO account needs more features: saved collections, personalization | The account only supports checkout, and even logged-in users get asked to log in |
| 2. Interviews | Users want those features | Users need a clear reason to create an account before new features will matter |
| 3. Contextual inquiry | The main opportunity is on the website | Many unmet needs are in the gallery itself, especially finding and understanding information while visiting with others |

Each phase changed the question I was asking in the next one. I started by asking what the account should do, and ended up asking what would make the AGO experience, online and in person, worth staying connected to.

### Phase 1 — Competitive analysis

I started by comparing the AGO's website with those of the British Museum and The Met. All three museums aim to connect people with art, culture and education, but they position themselves differently and prioritize different things.

- **AGO:** serves mainly local and domestic audiences through exhibitions, community programming, memberships and education. Its goals include visitor engagement, financial sustainability¹ and more diverse collections and programming², reflected in a site built around visit planning, tickets and memberships.
- **British Museum:** positions itself as a global public resource for preserving and sharing its collection³, with multilingual resources and extensive digital collections for a worldwide audience⁴.
- **The Met:** serves visitors, students, educators and researchers through online collections, publications and learning resources, with goals of inclusivity⁵, advancing scholarship⁶ and exceptional visitor experiences online and in person⁷.

| Area | What I found at the AGO, compared with both peers |
| --- | --- |
| Accessibility | More accessibility issues than either peer; short of its own commitment to full WCAG AA compliance by 2021⁸; fewer physical accessibility offerings⁹ |
| Form validation | No real-time error feedback or input masks; errors appear only after submit and don't say how to fix them |
| Checkout | No progress bar; hard to move back a step; little explanation of the ticket options |
| Account | Supports checkout only; logged-in users are still prompted to log in |

**Recommendations from this phase**

- **Accessibility:** run a full WCAG audit, fix violations, and expand in-gallery accessibility offerings.
- **Forms:** add inline validation, input masks and error messages that explain how to fix the problem.¹⁰
- **Checkout:** add a progress indicator and back navigation; frame ticket options as questions ("Are you 14–24?") so visitors self-select.¹¹
- **Accounts:** fix the login bug, and look into adding features like saved collections. I tested this idea in Phase 2.

### Phase 2 — Semi-structured interviews

**Goal:** understand whether visitors want more from their AGO account, and what would make them use it.

I ran five 60-minute interviews on Microsoft Teams, with separate guides for AGO account holders, non-account visitors and visitors of other institutions.

**Participants**

- 2 students who hold AGO accounts
- 2 tourism professionals who visit museums regularly
- 1 former museum member who travels to museums globally

Between them, they had visited the British Museum, Tate Modern, the Louvre and the ROM, which gave me useful points of comparison.

**Recruitment.** My original quotas required account holders with a recent purchase. Under time pressure I broadened this to account holders who had *used* their account to visit, and to one recent visitor of another institution. This ended up being useful, because even the account holders had used their accounts very little, which became a finding in its own right.

**Sample questions**

- "Walk me through the last time you used your AGO account."
- "What, if anything, would make you want to create an AGO account?"
- "What has made you not want to create one?"
- "How do you feel when you visit the AGO?"

**Synthesis.** I coded the transcripts and clustered the codes into themes such as motivations for visiting, global comparisons, account features and account-creation concerns.

**Deliverables:** research report, 2 personas (student account holder; tourism professional), and a before/during/after journey map for each.

### Phase 3 — Contextual inquiry

**Goal:** observe how people actually behave inside a museum, from admission and wayfinding to exploring exhibits, and which tools (digital or not) they use along the way.

**Changing plans.** My original plan, approved by my professor, was to recruit visitors in the AGO lobby. On my first day there, museum staff stopped the research and didn't grant permission to continue. Over the next few days I:

1. Recruited two participants through friends and family
2. Moved the sessions to the **Royal Ontario Museum** and **Nielsen Park Creative Centre** (about 60 minutes each)
3. Added a **Reddit netnography** of AGO visitor discussions, so I could still include AGO-specific perspectives

Running a session at the ROM also gave me a useful comparison. Its maps, multilingual materials, mobile tours and QR code experiences were noticeably easier to find and use.

**Synthesis.** I clustered observations into navigation, information seeking, technology, accessibility, account expectations and interactivity, then cross-referenced them with Phases 1 and 2.

**Findings**

- The AGO has limited educational/informational material available, online & in-person
- Mapping & match between mental and conceptual model facilitates exhibit interpretation
- Users are interested in learning a lot more from their museum experience, but it needs to be handed to them.
- Frequent preferences for availability of more paper materials, such as maps, within the AGO
- Issues with labels and descriptions
- Users are reluctant to create an account driven from privacy and security concerns
- Mismatch between expectations/understanding of the account and actuality (Heuristic #2)
- Users are frequently motivated by exclusivity
- Users enjoy delightful, interesting, surprising, interactive experiences and opportunities to reflect
- Users understand the AGO to exist within a social context
- Issues with technology on site, in a state of disrepair, poor contrast, available in English & French only, not accessible

### What I found

When I combined the findings from all three phases, five main insights came through.

**1. Visitors can't tell accounts, memberships and passes apart** *(interviews; confirmed in-gallery)*

Participants weren't sure if they were account holders, members, 14–24 Free Pass holders, or all three. Several didn't know whether the "Free Annual Pass" had to be renewed.

→ *Opportunity:* one clear page comparing account, membership and pass benefits, with pass eligibility, duration and renewal spelled out.

**2. The account has no perceived value** *(competitive analysis + interviews)*

> "I don't see much of a difference between having an account and not having an account."

People expected personalized recommendations, event updates and exclusive content. Instead, signed-in and signed-out experiences felt identical.

→ *Opportunity:* make the account more than a checkout tool by adding features like saved works, personalized recommendations, event reminders and visit history.

**3. Privacy concerns hold people back, but exclusive benefits can win them over** *(interviews + contextual inquiry)*

Participants didn't want another password to manage, didn't understand why the sign-up form asked for their phone number and address, and wanted more control over marketing emails. Even so, they said they would share their information for a clear, exclusive benefit, and even something as simple as Wi-Fi access can be enough.

→ *Opportunity:* only ask for essential information and explain why it's needed, offer two-factor authentication and clearer privacy controls, and pair sign-up with perks such as early exhibit access or exclusive digital content.

**4. Visitors want more information before and during their visit** *(contextual inquiry)*

Participants spent a lot of time looking for labels, matching objects to lists and searching for information on their phones. They were much more engaged when the information matched what was in front of them, as with the ROM's interactive maps.

Visitors enjoy delightful, interesting, surprising, interactive experiences and opportunities to reflect and consider reconciliation, repatriation, intersectional diversity, accountability & responsibility, and transparency.

&#91;Image: interactive, reflective or surprising museum experience.\]

→ *Opportunity:* interactive gallery maps, QR-linked object pages, audio and self-guided tours, gallery previews, and multilingual content.

> "Exhibition design is a simultaneously networked dialectical process, one which develops in a dialogue between verbal-conceptual and visual-representational rhetorical techniques. The focal point of this process of reflection is the individual and his or her intellectual and physical perceptual capabilities" (Bertron et al., 2012)¹²

**5. Museum visits are social** *(interviews + contextual inquiry)*

Participants rarely described visiting a museum alone. Their visits were usually family outings, trips with friends, travel, hosting guests or work-related visits. However, the AGO's current digital tools are designed for individual visitors.

→ *Opportunity:* shared itineraries, group booking, ticket sharing and collaborative lists.

### What I recommended

I organized my recommendations in the order I think the AGO should tackle them:

1. **Fix the foundations**: a full WCAG audit, better form validation, a checkout progress bar and fixing the login bug. These are relatively small changes that would help every visitor.
2. **Clarify the offer**: a single page explaining the difference between accounts, memberships and passes, and a sign-up form that only asks for what's needed.
3. **Give people a reason to create an account**: exclusive perks, along with saved works, recommendations and reminders.
4. **Bring it into the gallery**: QR code object pages, interactive maps and tours that connect back to the visitor's account.
5. **Design for groups**: shared itineraries, group booking and ticket sharing.

The final report was presented on December 6, 2025.

### Reflections

**What went well.** The interviews gave me rich qualitative data, including a lot about how people feel when they visit museums. Letting each phase shape the next also stopped me from recommending features that participants didn't actually want.

**What I'd do differently**

- **Arrange site access in advance.** If I had arranged permission with the AGO ahead of time, I could have observed visitors in the gallery I was actually studying. I now plan for site access as part of the research plan.
- **Recruit to the original quotas.** Speaking with more long-term account holders would show whether people who use their accounts more often feel the same way.
- **Widen the sample.** Five interviews and two observation sessions are enough to spot patterns, but not to draw firm conclusions. Next time I would include participants with accessibility needs and observe family and group visits.
- **Test the concepts.** The next step would be usability testing on a redesigned account and in-gallery experience.

**What I took away:** this project started out being about the AGO's account, but it ended up being about the whole visit. The biggest opportunity is connecting how people plan, explore, learn and reflect, both online and in the gallery.
