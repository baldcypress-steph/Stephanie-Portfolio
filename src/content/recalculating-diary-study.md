## 'Recalculating'… Diary Study on Navigation App Inflexibility

*We expected frustrated commuters to deviate from directions provided by their navigation apps - but most kept following the route anyways, because they didn't feel they had any better options.*

<p class="cs-eyebrow" id="overview">Overview</p>

### Why commuters stick with navigation apps that frustrate them

<div class="cs-snapshot">
<div class="cs-card"><p class="cs-label"><span>1</span>The problem</p><p class="cs-big">Navigation apps plan the fastest route, but real trips often don't go that way.</p><p class="cs-small">Delays, extra stops and local knowledge mean riders end up working around the app.</p></div>
<div class="cs-card"><p class="cs-label"><span>2</span>The insight</p><p class="cs-big">Riders weren't looking for a better route. They wanted an app that could adjust when their trip changed.</p><p class="cs-small">Most stayed on the suggested route because the app didn't give them another option.</p></div>
<div class="cs-card"><p class="cs-label"><span>3</span>The solution</p><p class="cs-big">Give directions by route instead of by a single bus, let people add stops, and explain delays in plain language.</p><p class="cs-small">We made five recommendations, and I&rsquo;m turning the key ones into redesigned screens.</p></div>
</div>

#### The context

Navigation apps are designed around the fastest route available, but people often deviate from these suggested paths because of local knowledge, environmental factors like the weather, or previous experiences with misleading app data.

Because these apps are rigid, they often miss the "on-the-ground" reality behind people's decisions and end up causing frustration. We wanted to understand **why and when people choose to ignore their app's instructions**, so that navigation tools could be designed around how people actually travel.

Our research direction came from an HCI study by Ju Yeon Jung and Tom Steinberger at KAIST in Daejeon, South Korea, called "Modes of Interaction with Navigation Apps"<sup>6</sup>. We wanted to build on their findings by looking specifically at the moments when people leave the route their transit or navigation app gives them.

<p class="cs-part" id="framing"><span>01</span>Background research</p>

### Navigation apps are built for people who follow directions

A CHI 2025 study by Jung and Steinberger found that people use navigation apps in three modes, and often switch between them in a single trip<sup>6</sup>. Most apps are designed as if every user is always in Follow mode. Our study explored further what happens in Modify and Background mode.

<div class="cs-compare">
<div class="cs-card"><p class="cs-title">Follow</p><p class="cs-tag">Hands navigation to the app</p><dl><div><dt>Behaviour</dt><dd>Follows each step exactly, with no deviations</dd></div><div><dt>Breaks down</dt><dd>Real-time changes are stressful; the map doesn't match the street</dd></div></dl></div>
<div class="cs-card"><p class="cs-title">Modify</p><p class="cs-tag">Works with the app</p><dl><div><dt>Behaviour</dt><dd>Changes the route, the order or the start and end points as things change</dd></div><div><dt>Breaks down</dt><dd>The app can't absorb the changes</dd></div></dl></div>
<div class="cs-card"><p class="cs-title">Background</p><p class="cs-tag">Mostly relies on themselves</p><dl><div><dt>Behaviour</dt><dd>Glances at the app for reference</dd></div><div><dt>Breaks down</dt><dd>The app offers little beyond an ETA</dd></div></dl></div>
</div>

<p class="cs-takeaway"><span>Key takeaway</span>Going in, we expected frustration with the app to push people off its route.</p>

<p class="cs-part" id="diaries"><span>02</span>Diary entries · Mar – Apr 2026</p>

### Most trips stayed on the suggested route

Only **6 of 32 trips** deviated from the suggested route. No one's scores rose or fell steadily over the two weeks, so repeated use alone didn't change how people felt about the app.

When we looked at why people opened the app, most of them already knew where they were going. They mainly wanted to know **when** their bus would come.

<p class="cs-part" id="hypotheses"><span>03</span>Synthesis · Apr 2026</p>

### Frustration was common, but it rarely changed anyone's route

All 6 deviations came from P3, who knows the Scarborough bus routes well. The other participants ran into the same problems but still stayed on the route.

#### Hypotheses, tested

| Hypothesis | Result | What we found |
| --- | --- | --- |
| H1: Less deviation in bad weather or at night | Not supported | Weather was a stressor, but it lowered satisfaction rather than suppressing deviation |
| H2: Misleading info lowers trust and raises deviation | Partly supported | Trust dropped, but only 1 participant deviated; the others felt they had no better alternative |
| H3: Most deviations come from local knowledge | Partly supported | True for P3 in Scarborough; for others, local knowledge shaped *how* they used the app, not *whether* they deviated |
| H4: Satisfaction varies with situational factors | Strongly supported | Delays, rain, crowding and other passengers drove scores, mostly beyond the app's control |
| H5: Apps show limits in flexibility | Strongly supported | Every participant described a moment the app failed to adapt |

> "So it's not really worth it to deviate from the route suggested by the app, because at the end, they're going to end up being more in trouble, end up spending more time, and it's not going to be worth it."
>
> <cite>P2, post-study interview</cite>

<p class="cs-takeaway"><span>Key takeaway</span>People stayed on the route because leaving it felt riskier, even when they didn't fully trust the app.</p>

<p class="cs-eyebrow" id="findings">What I found</p>

### Five things we learned

#### 01 Riders followed the route because they didn't see another option, even when they didn't trust it.

> Misleading information lowered trust, but 3 of 4 participants still followed the app. Leaving the route felt riskier than sticking with a bad one. *Diary entries · Interviews*

#### 02 When plans changed, the app didn't change with them.

> When a bus ran late, the app kept pointing riders to the bus that had already left. "The app was too specific and didn't really consider buses not being on time when planning the route, and doesn't update as you travel." *P3 · P4*

#### 03 Real trips often have more than one stop, but transit directions only allow one destination.

> Every participant had at least one trip with more than one stop. Google Maps lets you add stops when driving, but not on transit. "If I want a smoothie on the way to work I have to have two separate trips." *P3*

#### 04 Satisfaction depended more on the trip than on the app.

> Delays, rain and other passengers had the biggest effect on scores, and some riders just accepted that these were out of anyone's control. "The navigation app cannot predict what is going to happen in the future… it's beyond their control." *P2 · P4*

#### 05 People switch modes during a trip, and the app should be able to switch with them.

> For P3, that would mean seeing every bus going her way. P4 wanted proactive alerts, and P2 wanted the app to stay out of the way on a familiar route. *P2 · P3 · P4*

> "The app doesn't say wait here for the next bus going to Kennedy. It says wait here for like the 905 or the 133, but if another bus shows up before the 133 that's still going to take me to Kennedy Station, I'll just get on that bus."
>
> <cite>P3, post-study interview</cite>

We combined these patterns into two personas with journey maps. Benny, our primary persona, follows the app and double-checks it against a second one. Daisy, our secondary persona, changes her route as she goes.

<p class="cs-eyebrow" id="recommendations">What I recommended</p>

### Recommendations for more flexible transit directions

<div class="cs-roadmap">
<div class="cs-card"><p class="cs-num">01</p><p class="cs-title">Give directions by route instead of by bus</p><p class="cs-small">For example, "Take the 38 or the 116" instead of one bus picked in advance</p></div>
<div class="cs-card"><p class="cs-num">02</p><p class="cs-title">Tell people what to do when things change</p><p class="cs-small">For example, "Bus 29 is 10 minutes late, take Bus 35 from King Street instead"</p></div>
<div class="cs-card"><p class="cs-num">03</p><p class="cs-title">Let people add stops</p><p class="cs-small">Add stops before or during a trip and see the new ETA</p></div>
<div class="cs-card"><p class="cs-num">04</p><p class="cs-title">Show how often buses come</p><p class="cs-small">Simple icons for which buses come often and which only come every 30 minutes</p></div>
<div class="cs-card"><p class="cs-num">05</p><p class="cs-title">Show trip conditions</p><p class="cs-small">Crowding, weather, price, and the general "vibe" of the bus</p></div>

<p class="cs-eyebrow" id="takeaways">Takeaways</p>

### What I'd do differently next time

<div class="cs-roadmap cs-roadmap--3">
<div class="cs-card"><p class="cs-num">01</p><p class="cs-title">Ask fewer questions</p><p class="cs-small">Our form covered a lot of ground but didn't go very deep, and participants noticed the repetition. Next time I'd shorten the core questions, rely more on the rotating prompts, and offer voice or video entries.</p></div>
<div class="cs-card"><p class="cs-num">02</p><p class="cs-title">Read entries as they arrive</p><p class="cs-small">We missed chances to follow up on short answers while the trip was still fresh. I'd also test the form with a mock entry first and automate the reminders.</p></div>
<div class="cs-card"><p class="cs-num">03</p><p class="cs-title">Four participants is a small sample</p><p class="cs-small">Our findings point to patterns but can't prove them, and some participants changed how they used the app because they knew they were being studied. Next steps would be a contextual inquiry and a comparison of drivers and transit riders.</p></div>
