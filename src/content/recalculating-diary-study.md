## Context

Current navigation apps are designed to prioritize the fastest route available, however users frequently deviate from these suggested paths due to local knowledge, environmental factors like weather, or previous experiences with misleading app data.\
\
Because these apps are often rigid and fail to account for the "on-the-ground" reality that drives user decision-making, they can cause frustration rather than providing support. \
\
This study aims to understand why and when users choose to ignore app instructions, in order to design more flexible and adaptable navigation tools that truly align with the user's real-world needs.

The research direction for this project was influenced by an HCI study entitled “Modes of Interaction with Navigation Apps” conducted by Ju Yeon Jung and Tom Steinberger at the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology in Daejeon, South Korea¹. This project attempts to expand on their findings and look specifically at deviations which occur when users are travelling to a destination using directions provided by a transit/navigation app.

## Background

### Framing: three modes of interaction

Jung and Steinberger found that people interact with navigation apps in three modes, and often move between them within a single trip:

| Mode | How users behave | Where it breaks down |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Follow** | Delegate navigation fully to the app, and follow each step exactly (no deviations) | Real-time update can be stressful, mismatch between the map and the street |
| **Modify** | Users alter the routes suggested by the app by changing the sequence of interaction or changing the types of actions, or using different departure/destination points. As new circumstances arise and/or user preferences shift they make adjustments to the route provided by the app | The app can't absorb the user's changes. Reliance both on the app and their own agency. |
| **Background** | Glance at the app for reference, but primarily rely on their own knowledge | The app offers little beyond an ETA |

Most apps are designed as if every user is always in Follow mode. Our study explored further what happens in Modify and Background mode.

## Research Methodology

We utilized a ‘usage’ type of diary study, in order to document specific moments of interaction with transit / navigation apps. This study is not limited to any specific transit/navigation app. Entries were event-contingent: participants filled in the form right after arriving at their destination².

1. **Pre-study interview (30 min):** confirmed fit, set a baseline, explained the process and built a personalized schedule of 8 trips around each person's real travel patterns.
2. **Diary period (2 weeks):** 8 Google Form entries per participant, each sent by an assigned team member at the scheduled time. Each form mixed repeated Likert-scale questions with a rotating open-ended elicitation prompt, plus photo and screenshot uploads.
3. **Post-study interview (30 min):** an individualized discussion guide for each participant, written after a first pass of analysis, to probe their most notable entries.
4. **Analysis:** coding and affinity mapping in FigJam, descriptive statistics, personas and journey maps.

## Research objectives

1. To identify common challenges, breakdowns, and workarounds users experience while using the app in real commuting contexts.
2. Finding out what features and types of information users most want from transit/navigation apps during actual trips.
3. To examine how frequent use of a transit/navigation app affects users' perceptions of control, stress levels, and satisfaction with the app throughout the study period.
4. To understand what motivates users to deviate from the suggested route.

## Research questions

1. How does consistent app use affect overall satisfaction with transit applications over time?
2. How do external factors (for example, weather, or time of day) influence intentional deviations, or lack thereof, in participants' journeys?
3. When deciding to deviate from suggested routes, what are the most frequently occurring factors?
4. How can transit/navigation apps be designed to be more flexible / adaptable to the different needs and intentions of the user?

## Recruiting methodology

We recruited frequent navigation app users who commute regularly and know their routes, since deviation only happens when people have some confidence navigating on their own.

**Target audience**

- Young adults aged 18–30
- Studying and/or working part-time in Toronto
- Using a navigation app (Google Maps, Transit, Apple Maps, Waze) at least 4 times a week
- Travelling by car or TTC

## Participant overview

Four participants completed all 8 entries, giving 32 diary responses.

|  | P1 | P2 | P3 | P4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Mode of transportation** | TTC | Driving | TTC | TTC |
| **App(s)** | Google Maps + Transit | Google Maps | Google Maps | Transit + Google Maps |
| **Typical trips** | West Toronto to college and work | College, school, errands | Scarborough, home and work | Early-morning commute to work |
| **How they used the app** | "Just check and go" before leaving; cross-checks two apps | Monitors continuously, uses voice control | Checks once to pick a route, then ignores it | Monitors throughout the trip |
| **Interaction mode** | Follow / Background | Follow | Modify / Background | Follow / Background |
| **Notable context** | Newcomer to Canada; uses maps to learn the city | Experienced local driver | Deep local knowledge of Scarborough bus routes | Compares Toronto transit to home country with no real-time apps |

Trips averaged 55 minutes (median 46, range 25–120). Across all 32 entries, participants said they opened the app mainly to **check arrival times (16 entries)** or **find the fastest route (11)**, far more than to check unfamiliar routes (3), confirm accuracy (2) or find a comfortable trip (1).

## Sample diary study questions

Each entry had two layers: a core set of questions repeated every time, so we could track change across trips, better understand their context of use, familiarity and local knowledge of the area, their deviation rate/deviation drivers and their satisfaction. We also had one rotating elicitation prompt designed to draw out a different kind of reflection each day.

## Analysis and synthesis

### What the numbers showed

No participant's scores rose or fell steadily across the two weeks, so repeated use alone didn't shift satisfaction. Participants followed the app on most trips. Only **6 of 32 entries (about 1 in 5)** recorded a deviation, and all 6 came from P3, who deviated on 6 of her 8 trips. P1 described deviating often in his interview, but none of his diary trips captured it.

### Hypotheses, tested

| Hypothesis | Result | What we found |
| --- | --- | --- |
| H1: Less deviation in bad weather or at night | Not supported | Weather was a stressor, but it lowered satisfaction rather than suppressing deviation |
| H2: Misleading info lowers trust and raises deviation | Partly supported | Trust dropped, but only 1 participant deviated; the others felt they had no better alternative |
| H3: Most deviations come from local knowledge | Partly supported | True for P3 in Scarborough; for others, local knowledge shaped *how* they used the app, not *whether* they deviated |
| H4: Satisfaction varies with situational factors | Strongly supported | Delays, rain, crowding and other passengers drove scores, mostly beyond the app's control |
| H5: Apps show limits in flexibility | Strongly supported | Every participant described a moment the app failed to adapt |

### Insights

#### Inflexibility & inconsistency

> **When the app refuses to bend, users are left on their own.**
>
> Inflexibility and inconsistency occurred when the app failed to adapt to the user’s preferences, and when it behaved in ways users couldn’t anticipate or explain. When an app behaves differently across similar situations, by showing all routes sometimes and hiding other known routes other times, it undermines the user’s ability to build a reliable mental model of what the app will do.
>
> “The app was too specific and didn't really consider buses not being on time when planning the route, and doesn't update as you travel.” (P3)
>
> “When delays made me miss a connection, Google Maps didn't reroute quickly. It needed faster updates and better alternative routes.” (P4)

#### Waypoints

> **Journeys aren't straight lines, but apps design them as if they are.**
>
> Every participant had an instance where their trip involved more than just going from Point A to Point B, but the transit users had to calculate these stops by themselves, since the app has no option to add waypoints (intermediate stops built into a journey). Google Maps does support waypoints for car navigation but it is absent from transit routes. This does not align with reality: real human travel is filled with multiple destinations and detours.
>
> “I would add waypoints! Google Maps is fine for a simple trip but if I want a smoothie on the way to work I have to have two separate trips.” (P3)

#### Satisfaction is situational

> Satisfaction with navigation apps is not determined by app performance, but rather by whether the app helps users feel in control of the gap between user expectations and their reality. Some users have a stronger ability to accept that things are beyond their control.
>
> “The navigation app cannot predict what is going to happen in the future… it's beyond their control.” (P2)

#### Interaction modes

> **Users don’t interact with navigation apps in one way; they move between modes, and apps should move with them.**
>
> An app built with knowledge of the different potential modes could customize the experience for each user. For Participant 3, that would have looked like all available routes. For Participant 4, that would have meant proactive push notifications. For Participant 2, that would have been minimal interference with a trusted route.
>
> “Four or five different buses all go through and they all stop there to pick up people on the way to Kennedy Station because they’re all expresses. And the app doesn’t say wait here for the next bus going to Kennedy. It says wait here for like the 905 or the 133, but if another bus shows up before the 133 that’s still going to take me to Kennedy Station, I’ll just get on that bus.” (P3)

## Recommendations:

**Route-specific directions, rather than bus-specific.**

- Directions being designed for the specific route rather than the specific bus - so users can enter their destination, see all the options for bus/subway routes to take them to their destination, and then make a selection on the bus you end up taking.

**Meaningful instructions and feedback from app**

- Rather than showing raw data and expecting the user to interpret it, the app should provide specific, meaningful instructions.
- Consistent, updated, accurate information about collisions, incidents, disruptions along the route
- More accurate ETA calculations, especially for things like left turns
- Real-time info about which & how many buses are on the route

**Flexible waypoints**

- Users should be able to add intermediate stops to their journey before or during travel. The app recalculates the full route including the detour, shows how the stop affects the final ETA, and continues tracking from the waypoint once the user departs it.

**Icons to communicate**

- On the app, icons next to the buses/subways which can indicate how frequently the bus makes stops - if they have rapid departure times or not. So that users can be informed if a bus only stops for example every 30 minutes, so they can instead look for a bus with the icon that indicates it has rapid departure times.

**More details on specific bus/travel conditions**

- How crowded the bus or subway car is
- The weather conditions being integrated through real-time data
- The price of a trip being integrated into the app
- The “Vibes” of the bus (Participant 3)

### Further research

- **Drivers vs. transit riders:** P2, the only driver, had the highest and most consistent satisfaction. A larger study could test whether driving correlates with satisfaction, and why.
- **Ethnography or contextual inquiry:** several participants changed their behaviour because they were being studied (the Hawthorne effect). Observing commuters in context would capture more natural interaction patterns.
- **Mode-aware personalization:** test a prototype that adapts to Follow, Modify and Background behaviour within a trip.

## Reflections

The biggest surprise was how little people deviated. We expected frustration to push users off the suggested route; instead, most felt stuck with it because they saw no better option.

**What I'd do differently**

- **Fewer questions, more depth.** Our form gave breadth over depth, and participants noticed repetition. I'd cut the core set of questions and lean harder on rotating prompts.
- **Review entries as they arrive.** We didn't check responses closely during collection, so we missed chances to clarify sparse or confusing answers while trips were fresh.
- **Stronger screening.** I'd ask candidates to submit a mock entry first and screen out very brief responders.
- **Offer more than writing.** P1 found long written answers hard and would have preferred speaking. Voice notes or video entries would capture richer context and make the study more inclusive.
- **Protect the schedule.** Individualized send times meant late-night entries sometimes went out late. Automated reminders would remove that dependency on the team.
